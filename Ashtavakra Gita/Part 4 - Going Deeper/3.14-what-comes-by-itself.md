# Verse 3.14

> *Ashtavakra:* "For one who has dropped attachment, who is free of divided seeing and free of desire, a pleasure that comes of its own accord brings neither pain nor excitement."

So, what happens to enjoyment once grasping and rejecting have started to grow quiet? Well, this last verse of the chapter answers that question, and I think the answer is actually much gentler than we might expect.

Enjoyment doesn't disappear. It just changes its character.

Ashtavakra talks about a kind of pleasure that comes on its own. It's not something you chase, or arrange, or try to squeeze out of life. It just arrives, the way sunlight falls on your face when you step outside. And he says that for someone who has let go of attachment, that kind of experience is neither painful nor pleasurable.

Now, at first, that sounds a little strange. You might wonder, "How can pleasure not be pleasurable?"

What he means is that it no longer carries the old emotional charge.

For most of us, pleasure is rarely just pleasure. Before it arrives, there's craving. While it's happening, there's clinging. And afterward, there's often a little ache—the wish to have it again, the fear of losing it, or the disappointment when it fades. That's usually what we mean by pleasure. It's wrapped up in all of those extra movements of the mind. And in that sense, pain and pleasure always come together as a pair.

The free person has stepped outside that pair.

When something delightful comes, it's received fully and simply. And when it goes, it goes. Nothing inside them reaches after it, so nothing inside them is torn when it comes to an end. The sunlight is warm, and then a cloud passes over, and both are perfectly all right.

Now notice how beautifully this brings the whole chapter full circle.

At the beginning, Ashtavakra is almost marveling that we keep chasing pleasure even after hearing the truth. And by the end, we discover something surprising. When the chasing stops, pleasure isn't lost at all. Whatever comes by itself can still be enjoyed—perhaps even more completely than before—because you're no longer asking it to complete you.

So nothing has to be renounced, and nothing has to be pursued.

Things come, and things go, and you remain.

Let's just let the chapter end there, quietly.

What remains—the one who stays while everything else comes and goes—is exactly where the teaching turns next.
